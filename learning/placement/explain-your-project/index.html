Explaining your final-year project

A two-minute structure, the follow-up questions to expect, and how to show your own contribution.

← PreviousThe technical interview: what freshers are asked Next lesson →A 30-day off-campus job search plan

For freshers, the project discussion is often the longest part of the technical round. Interviewers use it to check whether you understand what you built — and what you personally did.

A two-minute structure

  1. Problem — what problem it solves and for whom (one or two lines).
  2. Solution — what the system does, in plain words.
  3. Tech stack and architecture — frontend, backend, database, and how they talk to each other.
  4. Your role — the parts you built yourself.
  5. A challenge — one difficult problem and how you solved it.
  6. Result and next steps — users, accuracy, speed, and what you'd improve.

Follow-ups to prepare for

  • Why did you choose this database / framework?
  • Draw the tables or the flow of a request.
  • How did you handle login and passwords?
  • What happens if 1,000 users use it at once?
  • What would you do differently now?

Worked example

Adapt it to your situation
"Our project is a hostel complaint system for our college. Students used to report problems on paper, and complaints got lost.

We built a web app where students log complaints with a photo, and wardens track and close them. It uses React on the frontend, a Node.js and Express API, and MySQL.

I built the complaint API and the database design — four tables: students, rooms, complaints and status updates.

The tricky part was duplicate complaints for the same broken fan. I added a check that groups open complaints by room and category, which cut duplicates by about half.

It ran in our hostel for six weeks with around 300 complaints. Next, I'd add notifications so students know when a complaint closes."

What to notice

• Follows problem → solution → stack → role → challenge → result.
• Clearly separates "we" (the team) from "I" (personal work).
• The challenge shows real problem solving, not just feature lists.
• Numbers (four tables, six weeks, ~300 complaints) add credibility.

Names, companies and numbers are fictional examples. Always confirm details on the employer's official site.

Key points

  • Problem → solution → stack → your role → challenge → result.
  • Be clear about what you personally built.
  • Prepare to draw the architecture and tables.
  • Know why you chose each technology.

Quick check

What do interviewers most want to learn from your project discussion?

They are checking real understanding and your own contribution, which predicts how you'll work on the job.

Practice task

Write and time your two-minute project explanation. Then answer the five follow-up questions above in writing, and draw your system's architecture on one page.

Interview corner

What was the hardest bug you faced in your project?

Describe the symptom, how you investigated (logs, breakpoints, isolating the cause), the fix, and what you learned. Keep it specific.

If you rebuilt your project today, what would you change?

Show growth: mention a design decision you'd improve, such as validation, security, tests or a better database design, and why.
